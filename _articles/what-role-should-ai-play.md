# What Role Should AI Play?

My Practical Delegation Test is a way to decide what work to delegate to AI, what should remain human-led and who answers for the result. It distinguishes three broad Delegation Modes: exploratory, assistive and executional use. They describe the kind of use, with different implications for what needs to be settled before work begins.

I recently explored these modes in my **One Concept, One Example, One Question** newsletter series:

- [Exploratory use](https://buttondown.com/philipbudden/archive/exploratory-use/)
- [Assistive use](https://buttondown.com/philipbudden/archive/assistive-use/)
- [Executional use](https://buttondown.com/philipbudden/archive/executional-use/)

This article applies those modes to work in social housing. You can subscribe to my newsletter [here](https://buttondown.com/philipbudden).

In exploratory use, the task itself is still being discovered. AI can widen the options, help make sense of unfamiliar material or challenge the way a problem has been framed. The person remains clearly in charge of deciding what deserves investigation and what work should follow.

Assistive use concerns a real, bounded task that a person continues to lead. AI might contribute a draft, critique an argument or help restructure material. Its contribution supports the work; core judgement remains human, and checking what it contributes needs to be meaningful and manageable.

In executional use, AI carries out a substantive part of a task as a delegate. That demands materially greater clarity about the intended outcome, a credible way to verify the work and clear accountability for the result.

If the purpose of the task is still being discovered, AI is helping with exploration. For a bounded task, we need to distinguish contributions to work a person continues to lead from a substantive assignment with defined completion conditions. The same tool can support all three modes, so to understand its role we need an account of the arrangement around the work as well as the output.

The wider test asks four questions:

1. Can I describe the outcome clearly?
2. Can I verify the result cheaply enough?
3. What judgement must remain human?
4. Where does the uncertainty go?

My earlier article on [the Practical Delegation Test in social housing](https://philipbudden.co.uk/posts/the-practical-delegation-test-in-social-housing/) applies the four questions to a repair case.

All four apply across the modes. The modes clarify what work is being attempted; the questions test whether the arrangement is appropriate and what responsibility remains. Naming an owner for uncertainty, the concern of the fourth question, leaves open whether AI was supposed to help discover the task or carry it out. Those uses need different accounts of success and different checking.

The first question has a different significance in exploration, for example. An unsettled outcome can be a good reason to use AI to explore the problem. Asking it to complete a task requires enough clarity to distinguish acceptable completion from a plausible answer. The framework warns against treating work suitable only for exploration or assistance as though it were ready for executional delegation.

## When an explanation becomes something to act on

Consider a housing provider receiving enquiries about service charges after issuing its annual statements. A service manager thinks the statements need rewriting and is considering a project to simplify them.

The manager could use AI to challenge that framing before commissioning the work. Given a carefully selected, appropriately handled set of enquiry examples, it might suggest that residents are unsure what changed from the previous year. Other enquiries could concern the explanation of the charge or the service residents believe they received. Those possibilities imply different investigations. A clearer statement won't settle a dispute about whether communal cleaning took place.

This is exploratory use. The manager is trying to understand what the next task should be. An AI suggestion can be useful even when investigation rules it out, provided it helps the manager recognise an assumption worth checking. A plausible explanation remains a possibility, however well it fits the examples supplied.

The manager would need to examine the underlying enquiries and talk to the colleagues handling them, drawing on residents' accounts where appropriate. They'd also need to ask what the selected material leaves out. Enquiries from people who contacted the housing provider can't, on their own, explain the experience of everyone who received a statement.

Suppose that investigation establishes a narrower need: improve the explanation of changes from the previous year. The manager can lead that work, asking AI to critique proposed wording against the questions residents raised. The manager still decides whether the explanation answers those questions. That's a different arrangement from using AI to help discover what needs investigating.

The modes imply neither a compulsory sequence nor a ranking of organisational maturity. The manager might conclude that a conversation with the service-charge team is sufficient, or that the issue is service delivery and a rewrite would address the wrong problem. Exploration has done useful work if it prevents a poorly chosen project.

The failure would be to circulate AI's initial possibilities as findings. For a management meeting, a paper headed “reasons for increased service-charge enquiries” invites reliance that “possible explanations to investigate” doesn't. If the evidence hasn't changed, the stronger heading hasn't earned its authority.

## A draft can contain a decision

Assistive use is valuable where the task is understood but doing it well still requires sustained human judgement. A complaint response illustrates why retaining that role involves more than checking whether the sentences are accurate.

A complaints officer has reviewed a case involving a missed appointment and the resident's unsuccessful attempts to obtain an update. The task is to prepare a response, and the officer asks AI to identify where their draft fails to address something the resident raised.

AI points out that the draft explains why the appointment was missed but says little about the lack of communication afterwards. The officer checks the complaint again and agrees that this needs addressing. They establish what happened and revise the explanation. Here AI has helped the officer notice a gap in work the officer continues to lead.

It could also propose wording that goes beyond the evidence. “We will make sure this never happens again” reads as reassurance, but it creates a commitment the officer may have no basis to make. Reviewing that sentence requires knowledge of what the organisation can actually change. A tone check alone won't reach the decision embedded in it.

Wording can settle a substantive question before anyone recognises that it's been settled. An apology can imply an accepted account of events; a suggested remedy can assume authority to offer it. The officer has to decide what the housing provider can commit to, including when further approval or investigation is needed.

A critique that reveals an unanswered part of a complaint could improve the response more than producing several pages of polished prose. AI can challenge something the officer has overlooked or taken for granted, while the officer retains the judgement and authority to decide what follows. Assistance should be judged against how well it helps that person examine and explain the case.

## Giving AI a piece of work to complete

There are also tasks where the organisation can settle enough in advance to give AI a substantive assignment. Treating every contribution as assistance can obscure the preparation and checking that delegated execution requires.

To review the service-charge statements, the manager needs to establish what residents were told before proposing changes. An internal reporting team is asked to prepare an evidence pack for that manager, covering the statement's accompanying letter, the published explanation and the standard replies used for enquiries.

The team selects the source documents and defines an assignment for AI: compile an inventory showing each communication's date and which residents it addresses, with a reference to the source and a short extract of any stated next action. Missing dates or unclear audiences must be marked as unknown. Assessing whether the communications adequately explain the charges remains outside the assignment.

That's a substantive piece of the pack's preparation, assigned under defined completion conditions. It illustrates executional use even though the result is for internal review and a person will accept it before circulation.

Where the information already exists in structured fields, an ordinary report may be the better way to compile it. The case for AI would concern material that needs reading across documents, and would still need to survive comparison with a simpler method. Choosing an appropriate delegation arrangement doesn't establish that AI is needed.

The test's verification question now concerns whether that defined assignment has been completed properly. The team needs to check coverage against the selected documents. It also needs to follow each reference back to the source, checking both that it leads to the right passage and that the extract preserves what was actually said. Checking a few convincing rows wouldn't establish that an entire document hadn't been omitted. The team also needs to know whether those checks are proportionate to the value of delegating the compilation.

A checkpoint could be appropriate where the source material creates an ambiguity. Two versions of a communication might require a person to decide which belongs in the pack before compilation continues. That pause doesn't turn the work into assistive use. AI is still carrying out the defined assignment, with a human decision at a point where the assignment needs one.

Compiling the evidence and deciding whether the housing provider communicated adequately are separate tasks. If the team wants AI to contribute to the assessment, it needs to examine that use in its own right, including the judgement and verification it would require.

This is a narrower opportunity than “automate the review”, but it could still remove useful preparatory work. The manager retains responsibility for interpreting the evidence and deciding what needs to change. Whether AI earns its place depends on the dependable work completed after specification and checking are counted, rather than on how quickly the inventory appears.

## Review doesn't tell us the mode

Human involvement tells us neither the mode nor how much authority has been delegated. Delegation Levels describe the degree of delegated authority and review burden.

In human-led work with AI support, a person controls the substance and acceptance of the work. Checkpointed delegation lets AI carry out work between points where a person reviews an assumption or approves continuation. Direct delegation still requires the person to specify, check and accept the work; choosing not to delegate is also a valid outcome.

Exploratory use usually sits within human-led work with AI support. Assistive use may be human-led or involve checkpointed delegation. Executional use may be checkpointed or directly delegated, depending on verification and retained judgement. These are typical relationships, not automatic allocations.

For the evidence-pack assignment, an effective checkpoint needs a person who can resolve which document to use, with time and access to the sources. For the complaint response, the officer needs to be able to withhold the reply or seek a different remedy. A review step needs a defined job and the power to stop the work progressing. I explored this more fully in [A Human in the Loop Needs a Job](https://philipbudden.co.uk/posts/a-human-in-the-loop-needs-a-job/).

## Agree what the next person can assume

My application of these distinctions is to state the intended reliance in the handoff itself, and revisit the delegation decision when that reliance changes. The fourth question asks who will absorb any remaining uncertainty. In the handoff, that means saying what the recipient can rely on and what they still need to examine, in light of the work AI was being used to do.

The manager should keep “possible explanations to investigate” as the management paper's heading until the evidence supports something stronger. In the covering note, they should identify which explanations have been checked and explain AI's role in generating them. The note also needs to state what the management meeting is being asked to decide. If the purpose is to agree an investigation, the manager should take responsibility for commissioning it and returning with the findings, and say so in the paper. “Human reviewed” would tell the meeting far less about what it can rely on.

If the meeting instead wants to authorise a statement rewrite, it needs evidence that a rewrite addresses the problem. Reusing the exploratory note leaves that evidence gap open. Its original mode hasn't changed; the proposed use now asks more of the work than the exploratory arrangement established. The manager needs to investigate before presenting the note as a basis for commitment, including reconsidering the problem if the evidence challenges the case for a rewrite.

Teams can agree simple defaults for ordinary, reversible tasks and give explicit attention to consequential handoffs, within their existing information and professional controls. A short covering note can be enough where it tells colleagues what the work is for and which checks remain before they can act on it.

The manager should show the person sponsoring the AI pilot what the exploration led them to investigate or reconsider. The reporting team should be able to demonstrate that the specified inventory was completed, including the effort needed to check it. Alongside any reported saving in time, that gives the pilot sponsor evidence to examine against the different work AI was asked to do.

Before approving the next stage, I'd want the pilot sponsor and manager to agree which of those expectations applies and examine whether the evidence supports taking it forward. A pilot that helped the manager choose a better investigation may have succeeded while remaining exploratory. That result gives the organisation a sound basis for deciding what to do next, without treating a useful suggestion as work already completed.
